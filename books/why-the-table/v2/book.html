How to read this draft

A table in Tel Aviv, on a Thursday, or on a Friday before sunset. Friends, or two people who share a home and do not share one kitchen. The room is a good room. It is not a kosher restaurant. This draft does not ask it to become one. One person at the table keeps kosher. The others want the card of the house. Today that table changes address, or it splits, or someone sits in front of a plate they do not trust. The dish this project is built to imagine is already closed when it leaves the kitchen that cooked it. The certificate on it, if the word kosher is ever used, is already the certificate of that kitchen. It arrives as one line on the menu. The rest of the table eats what the house cooks.

The draft has three parts. The first is the societal need in Israel, told only with figures that can be pointed at. The second is the Torah, limited to verses that can be quoted in full, on hospitality, on the table, and on love of one's neighbor. The third is the business: a closed line, a certificate that is already the kitchen's, and a restaurant that does not become kosher.

Three limits govern every page.

A figure is written only with a URL and a year. The share of couples who do not keep the same kashrut was not found. It is not replaced here by any neighboring percentage, including any share described elsewhere as "mixed."

A verse is written only as a real verse, named by book, chapter, and verse, in the English of The Holy Scriptures: A New Translation (Jewish Publication Society, 1917), checked on Sefaria on 23 September 2026. Where a Hebrew line is given, it is the Masoretic text returned with that same check, and only where the text arrived free of markup. No midrash is added. No practical ruling is derived. Questions of reheating, ovens, dishes, wine, bread, Shabbat, and Pesach are named so that they can be asked of the rabbi who holds the certificate. This draft does not answer them.

David Amor supplied the French question, “Comment apporter la Torah à la génération par amour ? Ça commence par les restaurants.” The English line in this draft, “How love brings Torah to a generation, it starts at the restaurant,” is an editorial rendering of his words. Leeya Katz has not approved or co-authored that wording in the material reviewed here. It is not a halakha, a verse, or the slogan of a certifying body.

The current market dossier and business plan use the same working name and the same refusal: the proposed pilot would buy existing sealed dishes from a supervised producer, subject to that producer's agreement and the exact certificate and handling rules. The project does not found a hechsher, does not sell one, and does not offer a new path in kashrut. Those papers also contain working hypotheses — prices, volumes, radii, counts of pilot rooms — that have no public URL. Those hypotheses are not copied into this draft. A plan can carry a hypothesis. A memorandum that promises a source for every figure cannot.

Part I — The need in Israel

A country in which keeping kosher is ordinary speech

The need is easy to picture and easy to exaggerate. The picture is the table that cannot order one card. The exaggeration would be a precise count of such tables. That count was not found. What was found is a set of public measurements of how Jews in Israel talk about kosher food, how they describe their own religious lives, and how the catering trade is certified from district to district. Those measurements make the proposed situation plausible. They neither measure the frequency of divided tables nor establish demand for a sealed meal.

The claim of an emerging need is therefore a hypothesis. To test it, a pilot would first interview diners with different practices who actually plan meals together, then observe whether a closed option changes their choice of venue. Restaurants would have to describe service constraints in their own words, and a producer would have to test handling and food quality. A dated count of relevant enquiries, menu selections, returns, and refusals could be compared with the pilot's baseline only after a protocol and consent are agreed. No such interviews, observations, or transactions are reported in this draft. Public population and certification figures below provide context, not a substitute for those measurements.

On the eve of Rosh Hashanah 5786, the Central Bureau of Statistics estimated the population of Israel at about 10.148 million. Of the Israeli population in that estimate, about 7.758 million were "Jews and others" (78.5 percent), about 2.130 million were Arabs (21.5 percent), and about 260 thousand were foreigners. The Bureau's own note says that "others" are non-Arab Christians, members of other religions, and people with no religion classification in the Population Registry. The figure for "Jews and others" is therefore not a figure for Jews alone. The same release says the data cover about 11.75 months since the previous Rosh Hashanah, and that the population figures are provisional, based on the 2022 census. The file carries a note that data on certain pages were updated on 15 February 2026. The release itself is dated 17 September 2025.

Source: Central Bureau of Statistics, Israel in Figures — Rosh Hashanah, Selected Annual Data 2025, media release 304/2025, Jerusalem, 17 September 2025, PDF.

The same release, in its population section for the end of 2024, says the population was more than 10 million. It estimates the population of Israelis at about 9.811 million: about 7.707 million "Jews and others" (78.6 percent of Israelis) and about 2.104 million Arabs (21.4 percent). Foreigners are estimated at about 260.9 thousand. In that same section it gives the self-defined religiosity of Jews aged 20 and over: not religious, secular, 42.7 percent; traditional, not so religious, 21.5 percent; traditional-religious, 12.0 percent; religious, 12.0 percent; Haredi, 11.4 percent. The release says the figures that follow, unless another year is stated, are for 2024. It does not, in the lines read for this draft, name a separate survey year for the religiosity row. The row is reported here as that release presents it, and not as if it were the same instrument as the Pew categories below.

Those five CBS shares describe how adults name themselves. They do not say who keeps a kosher kitchen. A person who says "secular" may keep kosher at home. A person who says "traditional" may not ask for a certificate when eating out. The names are the beginning of the map, and they are not the meal.

What people say they keep

The Pew Research Center's report Israel's Religiously Divided Society was published on 8 March 2016. Fieldwork ran from October 2014 to May 2015. In that report, Israeli Jews place themselves in four groups: Haredi, 9 percent of Jews and 8 percent of all Israeli adults; Dati, 13 percent of Jews and 10 percent of adults; Masorti, 29 percent of Jews and 23 percent of adults; Hiloni, 49 percent of Jews and 40 percent of adults.

Source: Pew Research Center, "Israel's Religiously Divided Society," 8 March 2016, overview.

These four names are not the Bureau's five. The years differ. The age frames differ. This draft does not add them, and does not "update" one with the other. They are two public ways of hearing how people in Israel sort Jewish life. Both show a large secular or not-religious share, a large traditional middle, and smaller religious and Haredi shares. Both leave the kitchen undescribed until a question about food is asked.

Pew asked that question. In the chapter on Jewish beliefs and practices, the Center reports that fewer Israeli Jews say they keep kosher outside the home (52 percent) than in the home (63 percent). The practice is described as virtually universal among Haredim and Datiim, and the chart language on the same page says "All Haredim and Datiim." Among Masortim, 86 percent say they keep kosher at home. Among Hilonim, 33 percent say they keep kosher at home, and 63 percent say they do not. Outside the home, all Haredim and Datiim in the survey, and 69 percent of Masortim, say they observe kosher laws. Among Hilonim, about one in five do so. The phrase "about one in five" is Pew's. This draft does not convert it into a percentage the page does not print.

Source: Pew Research Center, "Jewish Beliefs and Practices," 8 March 2016, chapter.

Two cautions belong next to these shares, because they protect the draft from a false precision.

The first caution is about subtraction. Sixty-three percent at home and fifty-two percent outside are two answers. Their difference is not, by itself, the share of people who keep kosher at home and abandon it in a restaurant. A person may answer both questions the same way. This draft does not subtract one answer from the other and call the result a kind of eater.

The second caution is about the word kosher in a survey answer. Pew explains that Jewish dietary laws include, among other practices, not eating meat and dairy together and not eating certain animal foods, including pork and shellfish. A respondent who says "I keep kosher" is reporting a self-understanding. The survey did not inspect the kitchen. The Israel Democracy Institute, in a later survey, separates "always," "usually," "only with a certificate I trust," and "by my own understanding." Those are different sentences. A business that needs one of them cannot quietly borrow another.

Pew also reports that 16 percent of Israeli Jews say they eat pork, and 82 percent say they do not. Even among Hilonim, 67 percent say they refrain from pork. Jews of Sephardi or Mizrahi ancestry are much more likely than Ashkenazi Jews to say they keep kosher outside the home (70 percent against 35 percent). Children of immigrants from the former Soviet Union are about twice as likely as their parents to keep kosher at home (50 percent against 24 percent) and outside the home (35 percent against 19 percent). The same chapter says that roughly half of Jews who speak Russian at home eat pork (47 percent) and roughly half refrain (49 percent). These lines matter because they show movement inside families and inside the category "secular," without ever counting couples who sit at one table under two standards.

The Institute's December 2024 biennial report on religion and state includes a public-opinion survey conducted in September 2024. In all, 1,211 women and men aged 18 and over were interviewed: 1,001 as a representative sample of Jewish society, and 210 as a representative sample of Arab citizens of Israel. The samples were weighted by religious self-definition, separately for Jews and Arabs, by age, and by share of the adult population. The maximum sampling error is given as 2.82 percent for the whole sample, 3.1 percent for Jews, and 6.76 percent for Arabs.

Source: Ariel Finkelstein, Ayala Goldberg, Shlomit Ravitzky Tur-Paz, and Yaari Padan, Biennial Statistical Report on Religion and State in Israel 2024, Israel Democracy Institute, December 2024, PDF; publication page, idi.org.il/books/59047.

In that survey, 68 percent of Jews say they are careful to some degree about eating kosher food: 45 percent say they are always careful, and 23 percent say they are usually careful. The report says the 2022 biennial findings were almost identical. When the question turns to a place of eating out, the shares change shape. Of Jews, 35.5 percent say they eat only at a business that has a kashrut certificate they trust. Another 19.5 percent keep kosher according to their own understanding, or according to what the owner states, even if the business has no certificate. The remaining 45 percent do not check at all. Among secular respondents, the report says a clear majority — 85 percent — have no interest in a certificate or in a statement from the business. Among traditional respondents who are not religious, 21 percent insist on a certificate, and 44 percent keep kosher by their own understanding or by the owner's statement even without one. Among Haredi, national-religious, and traditional-religious respondents, the report describes a clear majority who eat only at a business with a certificate.

Among Jews who insist on a certificate, 47 percent say they trust any rabbinate certificate, and another 18.5 percent trust a mehadrin rabbinate or specific rabbinates. The remaining third trust only private badatz certificates, and most of that third — 23.5 percent of those who insist on a certificate — trust only specific badatz authorities. The report notes that the number of secular Jews who insist on eating only where there is a certificate is very small, so a percentage inside that cell should be read with that warning. Among Haredi respondents who eat only with a certificate, 2 percent say they trust all rabbinate certificates, and 12 percent trust specific rabbinate certificates; the preference described is for badatz supervision, and often for one badatz or a few named ones. Among national-religious respondents who eat only with a certificate, 87 percent eat where there is a rabbinate certificate: 59 percent trust all local rabbinates, and 28 percent trust mehadrin or specific rabbinates. Among traditional-religious respondents who insist on a certificate, 90.5 percent trust a rabbinate certificate: 71 percent all local rabbinates, and 19.5 percent mehadrin or specific ones. Among traditional respondents who are not religious and who require a certificate, the report says almost all trust a rabbinate certificate, most of them any local rabbinate (82 percent), and a minority a mehadrin or specific rabbinate (13.5 percent).

The same chapter asks about Tzohar's certificate, among people who insist on a certificate. Twenty-seven percent say they trust it, down from 37 percent in the 2022 survey reported in the previous biennial. The report says the Haredi public rejects Tzohar's certificate completely. Among traditional-religious respondents who insist on a certificate, 29 percent trust Tzohar, while 71 percent, in the previous breakdown, trust any rabbinate certificate. Among national-religious respondents who insist on a certificate, 59 percent trust any rabbinate certificate and 47 percent trust Tzohar. When the Institute adds the people who keep kosher by their own understanding, 51 percent of national-religious respondents say they trust Tzohar's certificate. Tzohar is an organization that exists. This project does not adopt it, does not compete with it, and does not borrow its name. The figures are here because a seal has a public, and the publics do not agree.

Read together, the 2016 Pew chapter and the 2024 Institute survey say something modest and solid. A majority of Jews in Israel, on these measures, connect themselves to kosher food. A smaller share, still large, connects that practice to a certificate when eating out. A further share keeps something they call kosher without asking for a certificate. A large secular share does not ask. Inside the share that does ask, trust splits between the local rabbinate, a stricter rabbinate, and a badatz. Any plate that says "kosher" into that landscape has to say under whose certificate, or it says nothing a careful person can use.

The couple who do not keep the same kashrut

The table this project cares about is often a couple, or a family, or a pair of friends, who do not keep the same kashrut. The share of such couples was not found. It was looked for in the public sources read for this draft. It is not in the Pew chapter. It is not in the Bureau release. It is not in the Institute's kashrut chapter, which measures persons, not pairs. The companion research audit does not supply it. The market dossier in this repository already records the same gap: no figure was found for households or couples at different levels of kashrut.

That gap stays empty. It is not filled by the share of marriages across religions. It is not filled by the share of marriages across Jewish subgroups. It is not filled by any neighboring percentage a reader might have met under the label mixed.

What Pew does report, in the 2016 overview, is about spouses and friends inside the four Jewish groups. Ninety-five percent of married Haredim say their spouse is also Haredi. Ninety-three percent of Hilonim who have a spouse or partner say that person is Hiloni. Eighty-five percent of Datiim with a spouse say the spouse is Dati. Among married Masortim, 64 percent are married to another Masorti, while about one-third are married to a Jew they describe as Dati (20 percent) or Hiloni (15 percent). Nearly all Israelis in the survey who are married or living with a partner say their spouse or partner shares their religion. Two percent of married Jews say they have a spouse who belongs to a non-Jewish religion or is religiously unaffiliated. One percent of married Muslim, Christian, and Druze respondents say their spouse has a different religion.

Those are real findings, and they are findings about identity and about religion. They are not findings about kashrut inside the couple. A Hiloni married to a Hiloni can still sit across from a different kitchen: 33 percent of Hilonim say they keep kosher at home, and 63 percent say they do not. A Masorti married to a Hiloni may or may not keep the same table; the survey does not ask. Friendships tell a similar story of social separation without answering the food question. Only about half of Masortim (48 percent) say that all or most of their close friends are also Masortim. Among Haredim and Hilonim, roughly nine in ten say all or most of their close friends belong to the same Jewish subgroup. Among Datiim, 72 percent say all or most of their close friends are Datiim.

The need, stated honestly, is therefore this. The surveys show many people who keep kosher, many people who do not, and a great deal of shared life inside the secular and traditional majority. They do not show how often two people who love each other, or who simply want the same evening, fail to keep the same kashrut. Anyone who offers a percentage for that failure, on the strength of these pages, is inventing it. This draft declines.

The scene can still be described, because description is not a rate. A person who keeps kosher outside the home cannot eat the card of a non-kosher restaurant. Pew's 52 percent is the share who say they keep kosher outside, not the share who will accept any particular seal in any particular room. The Institute's 35.5 percent, in September 2024, is the share who say they eat out only where a certificate they trust is present. Some of those people will not sit in a non-kosher room at all, whatever is sealed on the table. The Institute's own breakdown says as much: a Haredi respondent who insists on a certificate is, in that survey, very unlikely to trust a general rabbinate certificate, let alone a dish served in a room that has none. The person for whom a sealed plate in a secular bistro is even a candidate is a narrower person than "anyone who keeps kosher." The working papers in this repository already say that the guest in view is someone for whom the rabbinate's certificate is already enough at home, and that a badatz public will usually refuse the room. This draft repeats that limit. It does not soften it with a market percentage it does not have.

A city where certification of the catering trade is partial

Tel Aviv comes first in the working papers because it is a secular dining city, dense with rooms that are not kosher, and because a single morning delivery can reach a cluster of them. That is a choice of where to begin. It is not a census.

A current count of non-kosher restaurants in the city of Tel Aviv was not found for this draft. The market dossier says the same: the count of rooms lives, if it lives, in a survey folder that is not this memorandum. What the Israel Democracy Institute does publish is older, and it is about a district and a trade, not about a street.

In the 2024 biennial, religious councils are reported to have issued kashrut certificates to 14,423 businesses in 2022, a rise of 5.4 percent from 13,683 businesses in 2020. Of the 2022 certificates, catering and hospitality account for 8,400 businesses, 58 percent. Food manufacturing accounts for 25 percent, and food retail for 17 percent. Of all those kosher businesses, 43.5 percent are in the center of the country: 26.5 percent in the Central district and 17 percent in the Tel Aviv district. The South accounts for 17 percent, and Jerusalem with Judea and Samaria for 16 percent. These are shares of the kosher stock. They are not the share of all businesses that are kosher.

On that second question the report is careful. It says the Bureau's economic classification does not match the religious councils' classification, so a precise rate by branch and district is hard. It then reports a special analysis, done earlier with the Bureau, of kosher businesses in 2019. On that analysis, one may estimate that about 66 percent of catering businesses in Israel — cafés, restaurants, and food stands — are kosher. The chart in the 2024 report is titled as the share of kosher businesses in the catering branch (restaurants, cafés, and food stands) by geographic district, for 2019. The highest share is in the Jerusalem and Judea and Samaria district, where 90 percent of catering businesses are kosher. In the Tel Aviv district, 49 percent of catering businesses are kosher. The report ties the contrast to the more traditional religious profile of the Jerusalem district and the more secular profile of the Tel Aviv district. It also notes that a restaurant or café open on Shabbat cannot, as things stand, receive a certificate from a local rabbinate, unlike a hotel. The footnote points back to Ariel Finkelstein and Gabriel Abensour, The Kashrut System in Israel: A Data Review, Israel Democracy Institute, 2021.

Forty-nine percent is a 2019 share of a district's catering trade, republished in 2024. It is not a 2026 count of doors on a Tel Aviv street. It is enough to refuse two slogans. It refuses "Tel Aviv has no kosher food," because on this measure about half the catering trade in the district held a certificate. It refuses "the non-kosher room is a rarity," because a 49 percent kosher share means certification is partial. The report does not print a separate line for the complementary share, and this draft does not invent one. Partial is the word the figure supports.

The same report describes Shabbat opening hours for culture and commerce, checked in a way that shows the city's week. It is not a restaurant survey, and it is not used here as one. In the Tel Aviv district, 23 cultural halls and theaters, 79 percent of those in the district, were found open on Shabbat. All 11 recognized museums in the Tel Aviv district were open on Shabbat. Of 31 swimming pools in the district, 97 percent were open on Shabbat, which the report glosses as a single pool closed. The checks are those of the 2024 report, with the museum and pool findings stated for the district pattern the authors describe. A room that serves lunch on Saturday is, in this city, an ordinary commercial fact. A kosher kitchen certified by a local rabbinate does not follow that fact. The project's working papers therefore withhold the sealed line on Saturday. That withholding is an operating decision of the draft, pending a written rule from the rabbi of whatever certificate is in question. It is not a ruling invented here about what Shabbat permits.

What people already do, and what is still missing

When a table cannot share one card, the substitutes already exist. They are named here without a volume, because no sourced volume was found.

The observant person can choose a kosher restaurant. The table then either moves there, and the others eat in a room they did not choose, or it splits, and the evening becomes two addresses. The person can eat at home, or bring a dish from home. A certified kitchen can deliver to the house. OU Kosher's guide to travel meals describes a further pattern already in use: certified meals carried by planes, trains, and ships, heated while double wrapped, with the caterer's and certifier's seals intact. The guide says the certification covers only the food in the sealed package; loose bread, wine, and other food served by the carrier are not included. It does not grant this project permission to use a restaurant oven. The proposed dish would require a separate written procedure from the supervising rabbi for its exact product, package, and service. The precedent shows an object, not an approval of this business.

Source for the travel-meal description: OU Kosher, The Kosher Primer, travel section, checked 24 September 2026.

The missing object is narrow. It is a way to remain at this table, in this non-kosher room, with a dish whose certificate was earned in a kitchen that already has one. The room does not change its name. The guest who needs the certificate does not have to leave, and does not have to pretend the salad, the bread, the wine, or the dessert of the house are covered. Friends who do not keep kosher order the card they came for. Whether enough such tables exist to carry a business is not answered by the 63 percent, the 52 percent, the 35.5 percent, or the 49 percent. Those figures show a public and a city in which the problem is intelligible. They do not show a forecast. This draft does not add one.

Part II — Torah: hospitality, the table, and love of one's neighbor

What a verse is allowed to do here

This part quotes verses. It says what they say. It stops where a ruling would begin.

The quotations are from the 1917 Jewish Publication Society translation, a public-domain text, as served by Sefaria for the version titled The Holy Scriptures: A New Translation (JPS 1917). Each quotation was checked on 23 September 2026. The citation under each block is the citation. If a verse was not in that check, it is not in this part. Rabbinic expansion — what later law made of a kid and its mother's milk, what later law required of a stove, a seal, or a Gentile cook — is real in Jewish life and is not retold here. Retelling it would be a ruling, or a textbook of rulings, and this draft is neither. Where a verse is followed, in the tradition, by a famous story that is not the verse, the story is left out. The reader who knows the story will notice the silence. The silence is the method.

Hospitality, in these verses, is a run toward the guest, a drink of water, a bed, a morsel, a place at a meal. The table, in these verses, is a furnished thing: a surface set before a person, a ring of children, a dinner where love is, a room with a table in it for the traveler. Love of one's neighbor, in these verses, is a command in Leviticus, repeated toward the stranger, and joined to bread for the hungry and to an open door. None of these verses names a restaurant in Tel Aviv. None of them says that a commercial sealed plate fulfills the command. The verses are here because David Amor asks whether love can bring Torah to a generation, beginning in restaurants. The verses have to be real, so that his question can sit beside them without being mistaken for scripture.

Abraham at the tent

Genesis 18:2. and he lifted up his eyes and looked, and, lo, three men stood over against him; and when he saw them, he ran to meet them from the tent door, and bowed down to the earth,

Genesis 18:3. and said: ‘My lord, if now I have found favour in thy sight, pass not away, I pray thee, from thy servant.

Genesis 18:4. Let now a little water be fetched, and wash your feet, and recline yourselves under the tree.

Genesis 18:5. And I will fetch a morsel of bread, and stay ye your heart; after that ye shall pass on; forasmuch as ye are come to your servant.’ And they said: ‘So do, as thou hast said.’

Genesis 18:6. And Abraham hastened into the tent unto Sarah, and said: ‘Make ready quickly three measures of fine meal, knead it, and make cakes.’

Genesis 18:7. And Abraham ran unto the herd, and fetched a calf tender and good, and gave it unto the servant; and he hastened to dress it.

Genesis 18:8. And he took curd, and milk, and the calf which he had dressed, and set it before them; and he stood by them under the tree, and they did eat.

Genesis 18:2; Genesis 18:3; Genesis 18:4; Genesis 18:5; Genesis 18:6; Genesis 18:7; Genesis 18:8. The Holy Scriptures: A New Translation (JPS 1917), checked on Sefaria, 23 September 2026.

The scene, in the words just quoted, is speed and food. He runs. He asks them not to pass. He offers a little water, a washing of feet, a rest under the tree, a morsel of bread. He hastens to Sarah for cakes. He runs to the herd for a tender calf and gives it to the servant to prepare. He takes curd, and milk, and the calf, and sets them before the guests. He stands by them under the tree, and they eat.

That is the verse. A later reader hears, in the curd and the milk and the calf together, a question that the laws of meat and milk will press. This draft does not answer it. It does not say the verse permits a later table to mix meat and milk. It does not import a story in which the guests did not really eat, or ate only one of the foods, or in which the foods were timed apart. Those stories are not these verses. Genesis 18:8 says he set curd, and milk, and the calf before them, and they ate. The sentences about a kid and its mother's milk are quoted later, where they stand in Exodus and Deuteronomy. How far those sentences reach is halakhah. It is not decided on this page.

What the scene does support, as a plain reading, is the posture of a host. The host moves first. The meal is made for people who were passing. The host stands while they eat. He calls the meal a morsel and then hurries a much larger preparation. None of that is a menu specification. It is the reason a project about a shared table is willing to be judged by whether the person who cannot eat the card is still a guest, and not an obstacle the evening has to route around.

Lot, Rebekah, and a meal that is urged

Genesis 19:1. And the two angels came to Sodom at even; and Lot sat in the gate of Sodom; and Lot saw them, and rose up to meet them; and he fell down on his face to the earth;

Genesis 19:2. and he said: ‘Behold now, my lords, turn aside, I pray you, into your servant’s house, and tarry all night, and wash your feet, and ye shall rise up early, and go on your way.’ And they said: ‘Nay; but we will abide in the broad place all night.’

Genesis 19:3. And he urged them greatly; and they turned in unto him, and entered into his house; and he made them a feast, and did bake unleavened bread, and they did eat.

Genesis 19:1; Genesis 19:2; Genesis 19:3. The Holy Scriptures: A New Translation (JPS 1917), checked on Sefaria, 23 September 2026.

Lot, in these three verses, rises, bows, and presses the travelers to come in, to wash, to stay the night, and then to go on in the morning. They would have stayed in the square. He urges them, and they turn in. He makes them a feast and bakes unleavened bread, and they eat. The chapter goes on, beyond these verses, into violence this draft does not retell and does not use. The meal verses are cited because they show hospitality as insistence: the guest who would have remained outside is brought in and fed. They are not cited as a story about Sodom's punishment, and they are not a model for a restaurant's floor plan.

Genesis 24:17. And the servant ran to meet her, and said: ‘Give me to drink, I pray thee, a little water of thy pitcher.’

Genesis 24:18. And she said: ‘Drink, my lord’; and she hastened, and let down her pitcher upon her hand, and gave him drink.

Genesis 24:19. And when she had done giving him drink, she said: ‘I will draw for thy camels also, until they have done drinking.’

Genesis 24:17; Genesis 24:18; Genesis 24:19. The Holy Scriptures: A New Translation (JPS 1917), checked on Sefaria, 23 September 2026.

Rebekah's answer is shorter than Abraham's table and just as complete. Asked for a little water, she says "Drink, my lord," and she hastens. When he has drunk, she draws for the camels until they have finished. The verse is about water, and about not stopping at the first request. A sealed plate that covers only itself, and leaves the bread and the wine of the house outside the seal, has to be as clear as her second sentence. The first kindness is not allowed to blur into a second kindness the kitchen did not give.

A table the house has already divided

Genesis 43:32. And they set on for him by himself, and for them by themselves, and for the Egyptians, that did eat with him, by themselves; because the Egyptians might not eat bread with the Hebrews; for that is an abomination unto the Egyptians.

Genesis 43:32. The Holy Scriptures: A New Translation (JPS 1917), checked on Sefaria, 23 September 2026.

Joseph's brothers are in his house. Food is served. It is served apart: for him by himself, for them by themselves, and for the Egyptians who ate with him by themselves, because the Egyptians might not eat bread with the Hebrews. Part III — The business

The closed line

The business is a line on a menu.

The proposed pilot starts with an existing producer, not a kitchen built for this project. If its current certificate covers the exact dish and the new route is approved, the dish would leave that kitchen closed. A restaurant that is not kosher would store it according to the producer's instructions, serve it under an approved protocol, and leave every other line alone. The guest who keeps kosher would decide whether this product and its supervision suit them. Friends could order the house's food. Sorotzkin's public FAQ describes direct delivery to households, with no physical shop; it does not describe this proposed restaurant channel. THE KOSHER OPTION ISRAEL is a working name, not a seal. Any menu wording must first be approved by Israeli counsel, the producer, and the relevant certifying authority; this draft supplies no ready-to-print line.

"Closed" means the seal is intact until the guest opens it, or until it is opened where the guest can see. A seal broken in the restaurant's kitchen removes that dish from the proposed kosher option. It is isolated and documented, not rescued with the house's ingredients, replated onto the house's china, or served under the word kosher. Return or disposal must follow a signed incident procedure; neither is automatic. This is a proposed operating rule, not a psak. A rabbi who supervises the kitchen may write a stricter rule, or may refuse the whole service of a sealed dish in a non-kosher room. If that rabbi refuses, the project stops.

The dish is designed to be eaten from its own container, unless the rabbi of the certificate has written some other handling. The house's plates do not receive this food on the strength of this draft. The house's oven does not receive it either, unless that same rabbi has written that a closed container may be warmed, and how. Until such a writing exists, the operating rule is that the oven of the restaurant does not touch the dish. OU Kosher's description of double-wrapped travel meals, cited in Part I, is a description of another supervised practice. It is not a letter from the rabbi of this kitchen. There is, today, no such letter in this project, because no rabbi has been asked. Service without reheating also requires the producer's safe serving-temperature instructions for the specific dish.

Wine, bread, salad, and dessert from the card are outside the seal. They are not covered by the certificate of the dish. The guest who needs them to be covered brings them, or does without. The kitchen does not complete the container from the restaurant's station. A visible seal is a condition of the product. A dish that can pass as an ordinary plate, with the certificate hidden in the kitchen, is a different product, and it is not this one. People will see the container. The draft accepts that. Hiding would be the deception the statute, quoted below, exists to prevent.

Shabbat is withheld. Restaurants in the city are often open on Saturday, as the cultural figures in Part I suggest for museums, pools, and halls, and as anyone who walks the city can see for dining rooms. This draft does not convert that fact into a rate of restaurants open on Shabbat, because that rate was not found. It does say that the sealed line, in the working papers, does not exist on Saturday, and does not exist on Friday after the hour the rabbi of the certificate sets. There is no production by the project on Shabbat, no delivery, and no reheating operated by the project. The restaurant serves its own card. The line returns on a weekday, on a dish that arrived before. Pesach is withheld in the same spirit. This draft does not treat an ordinary certificate as a Pesach certificate. If the certificate does not say Pesach, the line is not sold as food for Pesach. That is a refusal to invent a status. It is not a ruling about what Pesach requires.

Questions left with the rabbi of the certificate, and not answered here, include: whether anyone other than the producing kitchen may finish the cooking; what happens to wine and bread that are not in the container; whether immersion of a vessel is required for the container this kitchen uses; and whether any Shabbat reheating, by the restaurant's staff, could ever be written down as permitted. Until they are written by that rabbi, the operating rules above stay in force. A lawyer's question is separate, and it is stated with the statute.

The certificate is already the kitchen's

The project does not certify. The sentence is the whole of the corporate idea.

A plate may be called kosher, in public, only when a certificate that already exists covers it, and only while that certificate is true. The kitchen is the manufacturer. The certificate is the one given for that kitchen's production by a body the law recognizes and the guest can name. The restaurant is a place where the plate is held and served. It does not receive, through this service, a certificate for its room, its oven, its staff, or its card.

The current working plan names an existing deli, Sorotzkin / Jewish Deli, as a candidate supplier, rather than proposing a new kitchen. Its public FAQ says the deli is supervised by the Petah Tikva Rabbinate. The project has not obtained the current certificate, established which specific dishes it covers, or received the deli's or its rabbi's approval for service through a non-kosher restaurant. No supply is signed. The certificate, if the plan becomes possible, must be the producer's valid certificate for the actual product and route; it cannot be inferred from a website sentence or transferred to the restaurant.

The applicable statute is חוק איסור הונאה בכשרות, התשמ״ג–1983, the Kashrut Fraud Prohibition Law, 5743–1983. The Knesset's official law record lists Amendment No. 5, published on 19 July 2026. That amendment removed the private-body certification framework and changed the list of authorized certificate issuers. The numbered provisions below are a research reading of the amended statute, not an opinion on this particular service. Transitional provisions and their dates must be checked in the official text before any certificate or menu claim is used.

Primary sources: Knesset national legislation record for the 1983 law and Amendment No. 5, and the Knesset's announcement of the amendment's final passage, checked 24 September 2026. This draft is not a legal opinion; any menu wording or service procedure requires current legal and rabbinic review.

Article 1א states the purpose. In the Hebrew of that text: מטרתו של חוק זה לאפשר לצרכן של מצרכים שמבקש לשמור על דיני הכשרות ומנהגיה להסתמך על תעודת הכשר, ולמנוע הטעיה. Rendered in English for this draft, not as an official translation: the purpose of this law is to let a consumer of products who wishes to keep the laws and customs of kashrut rely on a certificate, and to prevent deception. A sealed plate with a story that is not the certificate's story betrays that purpose. So does a room that borrows the plate's certificate for itself.

Article 2, as now written, lists who may give a certificate for the purposes of the law. For an imported product, the Council of the Chief Rabbinate, or a rabbi it has authorized under the conditions the article states. For a food establishment, a place of slaughter, or the place of manufacture of a product, the local rabbi serving in the place where they are, or the Council of the Chief Rabbinate; and if no local rabbi is serving, or he is unable to perform this role, a rabbi the Council has authorized, provided he is a member of the Council or a public employee who is a rabbi in Israel. For the army, the Chief Rabbi of the army or a military rabbi he has authorized. The Hebrew of the manufacture clause, article 2(2), begins: לעניין בית אוכל, מקום שחיטה או מקום הייצור של מצרך או של מוצר – רב מקומי המכהן במקום שבו הם נמצאים או מועצת הרבנות הראשית. A kitchen that produces a sealed dish is, in this structure, a place of manufacture. The certificate the statute contemplates is the local rabbi's, or the Council's, at that place. This draft does not decide that a particular council's certificate, on a particular day, satisfies a particular guest. It decides only that the project will not invent a fourth kind of issuer.

Article 11 says that in giving a certificate, the person authorized to give it shall take into account the laws of kashrut alone. The considerations of a brand, a market, or a mixed table are not, in that sentence, reasons for issuing a certificate. They may be reasons for a restaurant to buy a plate that already has one. They are not reasons to print a new one.

The Israel Democracy Institute's 2024 report, already cited, summarizes a 2017 High Court holding in its legal background to the kashrut chapter: a business without an official certificate may not present any kashrut representation, and the owner may present a true account of the standards he keeps and of how they are supervised. The report gives the year 2017 and does not, in the passage read here, give the day. This draft does not add a day from elsewhere. The practical memory of that holding, for this project, is a warning about the word kosher placed on the wrong noun. A true sentence about a sealed dish is one kind of speech. A representation that the house is kosher is another. The 1983 statute as amended in 2026 is the text this draft uses for what is prohibited. The 2017 holding is cited only as the Institute reports it.

The restaurant does not become kosher

The statute separates, in its prohibitions, the food establishment from the product. The separation is visible in the text. Whether a particular line of menu type falls on one side or the other is a question this draft leaves with a lawyer. The market dossier already left it there. No lawyer has answered it for this project. The pages below are a reading of the sections, so that the business can be seen against them. They are not an opinion letter.

A food establishment, בית אוכל, is defined in article 1 as a place engaged in sale to the public of products, or in serving them, for eating or drinking on the premises, and also a butcher shop; for a hotel, the parts of the hotel where products are prepared or served for eating or drinking on the premises.

Article 3(א): בעל בית אוכל לא יציג בכתב את בית האוכל ככשר, אלא אם כן ניתנה לו תעודת הכשר. The owner of a food establishment shall not present the establishment in writing as kosher unless a certificate has been given to him.

Article 3(ב): בעל בית אוכל שבידו תעודת הכשר ובית האוכל מוצג בכתב ככשר, לא יגיש ולא ימכור בו מצרכים שלא ניתנה בעדם תעודת הכשר. An owner who holds a certificate, and whose establishment is presented in writing as kosher, shall not serve or sell in it products for which no certificate was given.

Article 4(א): יצרן של מצרך לא יציין על מצרך שייצר או על אריזתו כי הוא כשר ולא יציגו ככשר בפרסום או בדרך אחרת, אלא אם כן ניתנה לו תעודת הכשר. A manufacturer shall not mark a product he made, or its packaging, as kosher, and shall not present it as kosher in advertising or in another way, unless a certificate has been given to him.

Article 4(ב) says the manufacturer who was given a certificate for a product shall not market a product for which no certificate was given while marking it as kosher.

Article 5: העוסק במכירת מצרכים לציבור לא ימכור ולא יציע למכירה מצרך שלא ניתנה בעדו תעודת הכשר תוך הצגתו בכתב ככשר. One who sells products to the public shall not sell, and shall not offer for sale, a product for which no certificate was given, while presenting it in writing as kosher.

Article 6 is a defense. In a prosecution under articles 3, 4, or 5, it is a good defense that the accused, in good faith, sold or served a product for which a certificate had been given, or used a component for which a certificate had been given. The defense covers a person who relied on a real certificate. It does not repair a broken seal, and it does not cover a product that never had a certificate.

Article 9(א) tells the manufacturer or importer of a product that bears a kosher mark not to sell it, and not to offer it for sale, unless he has noted on the product the name of the authorized rabbi who gave the certificate, and, if a standard symbol was set, unless the product bears the symbol set in the certificate. Article 2א(ו) allows the Chief Rabbinate Council to set a symbol for each kashrut standard it regulates, and says that if it sets one, the symbol shall include a reference to the product being dairy, meat, or pareve. The official amendment published on 19 July 2026 (Book of Laws 3548, amendment 5, section 36(2)) expressly replaces the old reference to “the supervisor” (להוראות הממונה) in article 9(b) with “the certificate of kosher approval” (לתעודת ההכשר). The earlier apparent tension with the deleted definition of הממונה is therefore not a current textual conflict. If no standard symbol has been set, the dairy, meat, or pareve marking must follow the certificate as amended. See https://fs.knesset.gov.il/25/law/25_lsr_14184767.pdf, printed p. 789. This is a reading of the published text, not an opinion on how a proposed restaurant service would be approved. What remains clear enough to operate is this: the name of the authorized rabbi belongs on the product that carries the kosher mark, and the dairy, meat, or pareve character belongs in the symbol regime the text describes. Those marks are the manufacturer's to print, because article 9 addresses the manufacturer and the importer. The restaurant does not invent them at the pass.

Article 14, as amended in the תשפ״ו text: העובר עבירה לפי סעיפים 3 עד 5 , 7 , 7א , 9 או 10 , דינו – מאסר שנה. One who commits an offense under articles 3 to 5, 7, 7א, 9, or 10 is liable to one year of imprisonment. Article 15 extends exposure, in the circumstances it states, to an active manager, a partner other than a limited partner, or a senior administrative employee of a corporation, unless that person proves the offense was committed without his knowledge and that he took all reasonable steps to ensure the law was kept. The word "partner" in that article is the statute's word for a business partner in a violating corporation. It is not a word this draft uses for any kitchen or any restaurant. No such relation exists here.

The reading that follows is the reading already in the market dossier, repeated so that this memorandum can stand on its own. Article 3 speaks about presenting the establishment. Articles 4 and 5 speak about the product: the manufacturer marking it, and the seller offering it in writing as kosher. A non-kosher restaurant that wants to sell a sealed dish stays inside the second pair only if the writing is about the dish, and only if the dish in fact has a certificate. The restaurant does not present itself, in writing, as kosher. It does not present its kitchen, its oven, or its card as kosher. The line names the dish, the producing kitchen, and the certificate already given to that production. Whether a phrase such as "kosher option," printed on the card of an uncertified room, would be read by an inspector or a court as a presentation of the establishment under article 3(א), rather than of the product under article 5, is the lawyer's question. This draft does not write the menu line. It says the line waits for that advice, and for the certificate, and for the rabbi's handling rule. Until then, no public sentence says the dish is kosher.

What fits the text, once a real certificate is in force, is ordinary and strict. The restaurant buys the closed dish, holds it cold and apart, and offers it as one line. The line tells the truth the seal tells. The guest opens. Article 6 is the defense of the person who, in good faith, served a product that had a certificate. What does not fit is a sentence that the house is kosher; a seal opened and then repaired with the house's food; a warmed dish whose warming the certifying rabbi has not allowed; a broken cold chain still served under the word; a Saturday service of the line while the question of Shabbat is unanswered; a Pesach service under a certificate that does not say Pesach.

The guest's trust has the same shape. The room is not kosher because the container is. If this certificate is not enough for that guest at home, it is not enough here. The draft does not rename a rabbinate certificate to make it sound stricter. The Institute's figures in Part I are why. People who insist on a specific badatz will not be persuaded by a sentence in a memorandum. They should not be addressed as if they will.
